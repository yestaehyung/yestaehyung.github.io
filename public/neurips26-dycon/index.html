Personalizing mental health prediction as people change — without retraining the model

Seungwan Jin1, Taehyung Noh2, Junghyun Kim2, Uichin Lee1, Kyungsik Han2

1KAIST  ·  2Hanyang University

Phone and wearable sensors can track behavior for months, but what a pattern means differs from person to person and drifts over time. DyCon keeps a natural-language personal context for each user and evolves it with sparse self-reports, so predictions adapt to the individual without updating any model parameters.

01 / The problem

Mobile and wearable sensing offers a scalable way to follow mental health over weeks and months. Accurate prediction is still hard, because human behavior shifts in two ways at once.

Across people. The same sensor pattern can mean different things. Reduced mobility may be rest for one user and depressive withdrawal for another. The paper calls this inter-subject heterogeneity.

Within a person. A single user's behavioral baseline moves over time as their environment and habits change, which the paper calls intra-subject behavioral drift.

The usual fix is to personalize a model's parameters for each user. But labels in this field come from Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA), short self-reports collected in daily life. They are burdensome to collect and therefore sparse, which makes per-user fine-tuning impractical.

02 / How DyCon works

DyCon (Dynamic Context Modeling) personalizes through text instead of weights. For each user it builds and evolves a natural-language personal context with an LLM, using two loops.

Generate. From a session's sensor data, DyCon computes a correlation matrix across features and asks the LLM to write an initial momentary context: a short account of what this user's signals suggest, grounded in DSM-5.

Predict. The predictor estimates the user's state from the sensor data, the momentary context, and the longitudinal context carried over from earlier sessions. This first prediction is made before the current EMA answer is seen.

Refine (momentary loop). Once the EMA answer arrives, the prediction is compared with it. If the error is larger than a threshold ε, the refiner rewrites the momentary context, revisiting the correlation matrix for cues it missed, and the predictor tries again, up to Nr times. This loop addresses differences between people.

Update (longitudinal loop). When a refined context brings the error within ε, the updater merges it into the longitudinal context and records which traits it added, kept, or dropped, and why. That context is what the next session starts from, which lets DyCon follow a person's drift over time.

No model weights change at any point. The same LLM runs all four steps; the paper's main results use DeepSeek-V3.

03 / What we found

We evaluated DyCon offline on four public benchmarks: GLOBEM (547 participant-years, 10 weekly sessions), PMData, StudentLife, and LifeSnaps.

Bigger changes, bigger gap. Self-reported states are sticky: anxiety and depression scores stay the same in 48.9% and 53.2% of consecutive sessions. Simple baselines that reuse past answers are therefore competitive session by session. DyCon's advantage shows when a state changes sharply (FIG. 01). It also caught more users moving from a negative to a positive screen: 43.8% vs 28.9% for anxiety and 31.4% vs 21.2% for depression, compared with the strongest baseline.

Across study years. In GLOBEM's cross-dataset setting, where a model meets the same users in a later year, DyCon reached balanced accuracy of 0.547 for anxiety and 0.582 for depression, above every compared method, including domain-generalization algorithms and other LLM approaches.

Refinement is the piece that matters. Without the refine step, DyCon stays near chance in every session, even when the unrefined context is carried forward. With it, accuracy climbs after the first session (FIG. 03).

Beyond GLOBEM. On PMData, StudentLife, and LifeSnaps, DyCon's first-pass predictions had lower mean absolute error than the compared LLM methods in every session from 2 to 7, with no dataset-specific tuning. These datasets are smaller and shorter, so we read this as supporting evidence of generality, not proof of long-horizon scalability.

Backbone and threshold. Results varied by LLM backbone, with DeepSeek-V3 generally best among the three tested, and moderate thresholds (ε = 0.3, 0.5) beat strict ones: demanding near-perfect agreement before updating does not help the next session.

04 / Limitations

  • Noisy labels. EMA answers carry recall and social-desirability bias, which can flow into the contexts DyCon writes.
  • Privacy. Storing a running personal context raises privacy and ethical concerns; deployment needs privacy-preserving infrastructure even though no weights are updated.
  • Not clinically validated. The contexts are model-generated hypotheses. Agreeing with EMA answers does not make them clinically valid, and they need human review.
  • Selective updates. Sessions with large changes or positive screens are updated less often, so the retained context may under-represent them.
  • Offline only. All results are retrospective. DyCon is meant to support monitoring, not diagnosis; prospective studies with patients and clinicians are future work.

Further question

If the context a model keeps about a person is plain text, could that person, and the clinicians they trust, read it and correct it?
