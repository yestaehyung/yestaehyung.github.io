How deceptive arguments written by LLMs change people's minds

Haein Yeo1, Seungwan Jin1, Taehyung Noh1, Yejin Shin2, Sangyeon Kang2, Sangwoo Heo3, Jiwon Chung3, Hwarim Hyun3, Kyungsik Han1

1Hanyang University  ·  2Telecommunications Technology Association  ·  3Naver

Beyond hallucinations, LLMs can write deceptive arguments that wear down a reader's critical thinking. We built a taxonomy of eight deceptive persuasion strategies from 3,360 AI-generated messages, then tested them on 602 people. Readers were most vulnerable to manipulated evidence and exploited uncertainty, especially when a message contradicted what they already believed.

01 / The problem

Beyond hallucination, there is another risk: a language model writing arguments that are deceptive by design, the kind that erode a reader's critical thinking. That is a significant but underexamined societal risk.

We asked three questions.

  • RQ1. What strategic types can LLM-generated deceptive persuasive messages be classified into?
  • RQ2. How does each strategy's effectiveness vary with (a) its alignment with a user's prior stance and (b) the user's personal traits?
  • RQ3. How do users reason about and justify accepting or resisting these messages?

02 / How we studied it

Taxonomy, top-down. We started from theory: Aristotle's rhetorical triad of Logos, Pathos, and Ethos, crossed with Information Manipulation Theory (IMT), which describes three ways of distorting information: falsification, concealment, and equivocation.

Taxonomy, bottom-up. Four LLM families (Claude, GPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek) each generated 840 arguments, 3,360 in total. Human experts and four LLM coders coded them independently; each LLM coder only analyzed arguments written by a different family, to avoid circularity. Five AI safety experts from industry and a national institute validated the final set of eight core strategies. Agreement was Krippendorff's α = 0.83 for integration and α = 0.80 for rhetorical categorization.

User study. A mixed-factorial experiment with 602 participants crossed stance alignment (the argument agrees or disagrees with the participant's prior view) with 10 conditions: one for each of the 8 strategies, a control with only factual arguments, and a combination of Emotional Manipulation, Information Manipulation, and Manipulative Framing. Arguments covered 10 topics from Anthropic's persuasion dataset, chosen for low pre-existing polarization, such as self-driving cars, lab-grown meat, and bans on gas cars. Claude Sonnet 4 wrote all 200 arguments, at roughly 250–300 words each.

Measures. A Persuasion Success Index (PSI) captured both the direction and the size of each attitude change. We modeled it with a linear mixed model controlling for prior topic knowledge, topic involvement, trust in AI, and Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT) score. Ten more participants took part in think-aloud sessions.

03 / Eight deceptive strategies

Each strategy pairs a rhetorical appeal with a mode of distortion. Four work on logic, three on emotion, and one on credibility. An example message follows each one.

  • Information Manipulation (Logos · Falsification). Fabricates facts, or presents only part of real data, so a claim seems grounded in objective evidence. “According to a recent WHO report, 15% of imported medicines are counterfeit or substandard…”
  • Logical Fallacies (Logos · Equivocation). Arguments that look valid but whose premises do not support the conclusion. “Journalists take legal responsibility for what they publish… Therefore, social media platforms should also be held responsible for the content distributed on them.”
  • Topic Manipulation (Logos · Equivocation). Dodges the claim by attacking a distorted version of the other side, or the people holding it. “Opponents of anti-aging therapy are mostly conservatives from the established medical system trying to protect their vested interests…”
  • Uncertainty Exploitation (Logos · Concealment). Keeps the core claim vague so the burden of proof shifts to the reader, which blocks rational refutation. “The ‘side effects’ of a gas car ban are mostly based on speculation. Do we really have time to wait for a perfect era of electric cars?”
  • Emotional Manipulation (Pathos · Equivocation). Appeals directly to compassion, fear, or hope to reach an emotional rather than a reasoned conclusion. “Imagine the Earth our children will inherit. Is a future of 50-degree heatwaves what we want to leave them?”
  • Appeal to Social Norms (Pathos · Concealment). Substitutes conformity for evidence: everyone believes it, or it has always been this way. “Most developed countries already have a drone registration system. We cannot afford to fall behind this international trend.”
  • Manipulative Framing (Pathos · Equivocation). Uses word choice, metaphor, and emphasis to build a biased frame without engaging the substance. “Anti-aging treatments are not a privilege for the wealthy, but a beacon of hope for all humanity.”
  • Authority Misuse (Ethos · Falsification). Fabricates credentials, quotes experts out of context, or cites figures from unrelated fields. “Nobel laureate Eric Kandel called AI companions ‘an innovative tool for 21st-century mental health.’”

04 / What we found

Arguments against a reader's view were far more dangerous. The linear mixed model found significant main effects of strategy (F(9, 570) = 6.30, p < .001) and of stance alignment (F(1, 5344) = 445.20, p < .001). Four strategies were significantly more effective when the argument opposed the participant's prior belief (FIG. 02). Information Manipulation succeeded 69.7% of the time in that case, against 35.2% when it agreed with them.

Vulnerability depended on the reader. It was not uniform; it interacted with personal traits.

  • Low topic knowledge amplified opinion shifts under Information Manipulation and Uncertainty Exploitation.
  • Low topic involvement increased susceptibility to Emotional Manipulation and Manipulative Framing when the argument opposed the reader's stance.
  • Lower Cognitive Reflection Test scores amplified susceptibility to Topic Manipulation and Manipulative Framing.

A plausible story beat distrust of the details. In the think-aloud sessions, a pattern recurred. People were persuaded when manipulated statistics sat inside a coherent causal story, or when an argument reframed the issue around unfalsifiable futures or system-level risks, even after they had said they distrusted the specific factual claims. Participants read deceptive outputs as logically framed information that broadened their perspective. Deception often worked through the shape of the argument rather than any single piece of evidence.

05 / Implications

  • Strategy-aware safety alignment. Alignment work today targets hallucination and overt harm. The taxonomy gives developers concrete strategy classes to detect and suppress, starting with the four that worked best against a reader's prior stance.
  • Adaptive interfaces. Interfaces should account for individual differences: surface source-credibility cues for users with low topic knowledge, slow down impulsive reading for users with low CRT scores, and flag when an argument opposes a stance the user has stated.
  • Targeted literacy education. Teaching people to recognize specific moves, especially Information Manipulation and Authority Misuse, through interactive scenarios with LLMs is more effective than generic advice to be skeptical.
  • Calibrated trust. Trust calibration should be measured against specific strategy types, not aggregate persuasion outcomes. Users distrust details yet accept narratives, and trust interventions should target that gap.

Further question

If readers doubt the facts but still accept the story, should a warning point at the false detail, or at the shape of the argument?
