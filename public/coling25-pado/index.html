Detecting personality traits in text by comparing two opposing AI agents

Haein Yeo1, Taehyung Noh1, Seungwan Jin2, Kyungsik Han1,2

1Department of Artificial Intelligence, Hanyang University  ·  2Department of Data Science, Hanyang University

Asking one language model to read a text and name the writer's personality tends to repeat the model's own biases. PADO builds two agents for each Big Five trait, one leaning toward the high end and one toward the low end, and has a third agent judge which reading fits the text better. The comparison is more accurate than standard prompting across four LLMs, with no extra training.

01 / The problem

Knowing someone's personality helps in understanding their context and in personalizing services, so researchers have long tried to infer it from what people write. The task is hard for three reasons.

Traits are latent and relative. Personality is not stated in a text; it shows up as tendencies that only make sense in comparison with other people.

Word cues depend on context. The same vocabulary can point to different traits depending on how and where it is used.

Labeled data is scarce. High-quality personality annotations are expensive to collect.

Prompting a single LLM for a verdict inherits that model's biases and tends to over-rely on surface keywords. Adding more agents helps little if they are prompted alike: similar agents reach similar verdicts, a multi-agent version of confirmation bias.

02 / How PADO works

PADO (Personality-induced multi-Agent framework for Detecting OCEAN) builds the disagreement in on purpose. The paper presents it as the first LLM-based multi-agent framework for personality detection. It runs the same three phases separately for each of the Big Five traits: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism.

Phase 1: personality induction. PADO creates two reasoner agents per trait, one induced toward the high pole and one toward the low pole, using the Personality Prompting (P²) method. The induced agents were checked for internal consistency on the MPI evaluation dataset.

Phase 2: psycholinguistic explanation. Each reasoner analyzes the text along three axes from Pennebaker's psycholinguistic framework: emotional (affective tone), cognitive (reasoning complexity), and social (interpersonal orientation). It writes an evidence-grounded explanation rather than a label.

Phase 3: comparative assessment. A judge agent compares the two explanations, noting where they agree and how well each matches the text, decides which one better fits the writer, and gives a final high or low verdict for the trait. This follows the LLM-as-a-judge pattern, applied to a task where the answer rests on relative rather than absolute evidence.

Why contrast helps. Each trait has its own ambiguities, and the paired agents put them on the table. For agreeableness, the contrast helps separate genuine empathy from formulaic politeness. For neuroticism, it keeps situational venting from being read as a stable trait.

03 / What we found

We tested PADO on two benchmarks. Essays (Pennebaker & King, 1999) has 2,468 stream-of-consciousness essays of about 50 sentences each, labeled high or low on each trait by self-report; 10% were sampled for testing. MyPersonality (Celli et al., 2013) has 9,913 Facebook status messages from 250 users with the same kind of labels.

We compared PADO with zero-shot, one-shot, and chain-of-thought prompting on four LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-3.5-turbo, Solar-10.7B-Instruct, and LLaMA3-8B-Instruct), and with fine-tuned BERT and RoBERTa encoders.

Higher accuracy on GPT-4o. On Essays, PADO raised average F1 to 0.66, from 0.53 for zero-shot, 0.51 for one-shot, and 0.54 for chain-of-thought prompting. The largest gains were on conscientiousness and extraversion (FIG. 02). On MyPersonality, PADO reached 0.83 F1 on openness, above all baselines.

Not just a large-model effect. The smaller models, Solar-10.7B-Instruct and LLaMA3-8B-Instruct, improved in the same direction, with particularly large gains. That points to the comparative structure, not model scale, as the source of the improvement.

No training needed. PADO works entirely through prompting, so it captures implicit, relative trait signals without fine-tuning on scarce labeled data.
